The Northgate Archive: A Short History of Latch Encoding

Mira Delaunay began her career at the Coastal Institute in 1978, working on signal
compression for weather buoys. She was known less for her own publications than for
the students she trained. Among them was Tomas Ek, who joined her group in 1983 and
stayed for six years. Delaunay passed on to Ek a habit she had picked up from her
own supervisor: never trust a measurement that cannot be reproduced from its own record.

Ek left the Coastal Institute in 1989 and founded Northgate Lab in the town of
Ashfield. Northgate was small, never more than twenty people, and it survived on
contracts from harbour authorities. Its first hire was Priya Raman, a graduate of the
Ashfield Polytechnic who had written her thesis on error correction in noisy channels.
Raman spent eleven years at Northgate and became its head of research in 1996.

The work Northgate is remembered for is Latch Encoding. Raman and Ek developed it
between 1993 and 1997 as a way of storing sensor readings so that any corrupted
segment could be reconstructed from the segments around it. The idea came directly
from Delaunay's rule about reproducible measurements, though neither Raman nor Ek
described it that way at the time. Latch Encoding was published in 1997 and licensed
freely to anyone who asked.

Latch Encoding made the Orrery system possible. Orrery was a harbour monitoring
platform built by Northgate between 1998 and 2001, and it was the first system that
could keep running through a storm without losing its record of what had happened
during the storm. Orrery was deployed at Port Halbrook in 2002 and remained in
service there for nineteen years. Port Halbrook credited Orrery with the change in
its accident statistics after 2003.

Priya Raman left Northgate in 2001, shortly before the Port Halbrook deployment. She
joined Vellum Instruments, a manufacturer of marine sensors based two hundred
kilometres up the coast. Vellum licensed Latch Encoding from Northgate in 2004 and
built it into every product it shipped from 2006 onwards. Raman became Vellum's chief
engineer in 2009 and retired in 2018.

Tomas Ek stayed at Northgate until it closed in 2011. In its last years the lab took
on a single student at a time, a practice Ek had copied from Delaunay. The last of
them was Julian Adeyemi, who arrived in 2008 and worked on extending Latch Encoding
to video. Adeyemi never finished that work at Northgate. He took it to the Coastal
Institute in 2012, which is where Delaunay had started thirty-four years earlier, and
completed it there in 2015 under the name Latch-V.

Latch-V is used today in three places: the Orrery successor system at Port Halbrook,
the Vellum sensor line, and the Coastal Institute's own buoy network. All three trace
back through Latch Encoding to a rule about reproducible measurements that Mira
Delaunay never wrote down.

Delaunay died in 2007. The Northgate Archive, held at the Ashfield Polytechnic
library, contains her correspondence with Ek between 1989 and 2006, Raman's laboratory
notebooks from the Latch Encoding period, and the full deployment record of Orrery at
Port Halbrook. The archive was catalogued by Julian Adeyemi in 2013 and has been open
to researchers since 2014.

Two disputes are recorded in the archive. The first, in 1999, was between Ek and the
Ashfield Polytechnic over whether Northgate could use the Polytechnic's name in its
contracts; it was settled out of court. The second, in 2005, was between Northgate and
Vellum Instruments over the scope of the Latch Encoding licence. Raman, by then at
Vellum, argued against her former employer. The dispute was resolved when Ek agreed to
widen the licence, and the two organisations continued to work together afterwards.

Other holdings of the archive

The Ashfield Polytechnic library acquired three further collections alongside the
Northgate material, and researchers frequently confuse them. The first is the Halvard
Collection, donated in 1994 by the estate of Ingrid Halvard, a metallurgist who spent
her career at the Ashfield Foundry and had no connection to Northgate Lab. It contains
her working notes on corrosion in harbour fittings, a subject that overlaps with
Orrery's deployment site without overlapping with Orrery itself. Halvard corresponded
briefly with the Coastal Institute in 1981, though the letters concern a materials
supply contract and nothing else.

The second is the Ashfield Polytechnic instructional collection, which holds the working
papers of Professor Alan Broch, who ran the Polytechnic's signals programme from 1975
to 1998. Broch supervised Priya Raman's undergraduate thesis. He never worked at
Northgate, never met Mira Delaunay, and his notes make no reference to Latch Encoding.
Researchers looking for the origins of Latch Encoding often start with Broch because
of the Raman connection and the word "signals", and the archive catalogue carries a
note warning against this.

The third is the Port Halbrook Harbour Board minute books, transferred in 2016 when
the Board reorganised. These run from 1954 to 2014 and record every procurement
decision the Board made, including the Orrery contract in 2002. The minutes for the
Orrery decision are unusually thin: two paragraphs recording that the Board accepted a
recommendation, with no discussion of what the system did or where the technology had
come from. The technical evaluation the Board relied on was prepared by an outside
consultant, Denis Farrow, and no copy of it survives in the minute books.

Storm records and the reason Orrery mattered

Port Halbrook sits at the mouth of a tidal estuary and takes the weather from the
south-west. Between 1980 and 2002 the harbour lost monitoring data during eleven
named storms; in four of those the loss covered the exact hours when the harbour most
needed the record. The Board's own review in 1999 concluded that the problem was not
the sensors, which usually survived, but the recording chain behind them, which did
not. That review is the reason the Board went looking for a replacement system, and
it is quoted in the minutes for the 2002 decision.

The 1999 review was written by the Harbour Board's engineering officer, Clare Whitlow.
Whitlow had no contact with Northgate Lab and recommended no specific supplier. Her
report describes the requirement in general terms: a monitoring system that keeps its
record intact when part of the record is damaged. That description matches what Latch
Encoding does, but Whitlow arrived at it from the harbour's failures rather than from
any knowledge of the technology.

Cataloguing notes

Julian Adeyemi's 2013 catalogue divides the Northgate material into four series.
Series A holds Ek's correspondence. Series B holds Raman's notebooks. Series C holds
the Orrery deployment records, including the maintenance logs Port Halbrook returned
to Northgate each year until 2011. Series D holds administrative papers, including
both disputes and the terms of the 2004 Vellum licence. Adeyemi's introduction to the
catalogue notes that Series B and Series C were originally stored together and that
the separation is his, not Northgate's.

The catalogue also lists what is absent. There is no record of the Coastal Institute
years beyond what Delaunay's letters mention in passing, no copy of Delaunay's own
doctoral work, and nothing at all from the period between Raman's departure in 2001
and the Vellum licence in 2004. Adeyemi describes that three-year gap as the single
largest hole in the collection.
